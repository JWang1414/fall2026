When measuring the events inside of the bubble chamber, the manual defines the concept of the "invariant mass"
$$
	m^{2} = \left( \sum E_{i} \right)^{2} - \left( \sum \vec{p}_{i} \right)^{2}
$$
The momentum is known, because it is what we are measuring inside of the tank with Traxis. The initial energy can be determined with mass-energy equivalence, since we know the incoming particles ($\pi^+$ and $p^+$).

Presumably, the $m$ I compute from the pion and proton will stay constant throughout the entire event, so the masses of the resultant particles can be determined with something like:
$$
	\left( \sum E_{k} \right)^{2} = \left( \sum E_{i} \right)^{2} - \left( \sum \vec{p}_{i} \right)^{2} + \left( \sum \vec{p}_{k} \right)^{2} 
$$

The primary strong interactions that occur in the bubble chamber are $\pi^++p$. Both of these particles have a charge $+1$. Inside of the tank, positively charged particles curve downwards. Neutral particles will not show up, so the negative and positive charges will all appear in the bubble chamber.

A very clear strong interaction can be seen in 10-164-0886, rake 4. There are 6 prongs, 2 upwards, and 4 downwards. The net charge is $+2$, maintaining the original charge.

Another can be seen in 20-254-0792, before rake 0. Once again, this has 6 prongs. 2 curve upwards, 4 curve downwards.

In this same image, there is another strong interaction at rake 5. It has 4 prongs. 1 up, 3 down. Charge is still conserved

Generally speaking, the number of prongs that appears doesn't appear to be larger than 6. 4 and 6 pronged events are possible because of cancelled +1 and -1 charges. It could also be possible to have less prongs in the case of rarer events where particles have $\pm2$ charge. For example, perhaps a -1, +1, +2 combination in a 3-pronged event.

$N$ would be limited by the energy of the two particles. They cannot generate more particles from nothing.
