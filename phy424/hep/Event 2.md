# Top prong
There are no standout choices for this prong. However, the optical densities for the two particles in the top prong do not reach the same levels as the proton in event 1. Which leads me to the initial guess of a $\pi^+\pi^-$ decay. I will test this with the $K^0_{S}$, just like I did for event 1

Compute the energy from the two pions.
$$
	\sqrt{ p^{2}_{+}+m^{2}_{+} }+\sqrt{ p^{2}_{-}+m^{2}_{-} } \approx \sqrt{804.70^{2}+139.7^{2}}+\sqrt{67.06^{2}+139.7^{2}} \approx 971.7
$$
Compute the energy from the $K^0$
$$
	\sqrt{ p^{2}_{K}+m^{2}_{K} } \approx \sqrt{838.71^{2}+497.7^{2}} \approx 975.3
$$
Similar numbers. So this guess abides by energy conservation.

Now for the invariant mass:
$$
	m^{2} = \left( \sum E_{i} \right)^{2} - \left( \sum \vec{p}_{i} \right)^{2}
$$
Which in this case is:
$$
	\sum \vec{p}_{i} \approx \begin{bmatrix}
	778.55 \\
	203.48
	\end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix}
	15.98 \\
	65.13
	\end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix}
	794.53 \\
	268.61
	\end{bmatrix}
$$
$$
	m^{2} \approx 971.7^{2} - \begin{bmatrix}
	794.53 \\
	268.61
	\end{bmatrix}^{2} \approx 240772
$$
Converting the units, this is $m^{2}=0.241$, which is very close to the expected value of 0.248
# The total momentum
The measured momentum of the $\pi^-$ of the incoming beam is 9550.38 MeV/c. The total momentum from the resulting particles is around 1884.09 or 1982.17 MeV/c.
- These two values originate from two possible measurements of the momentum. However, they are quite similar

The large discrepency in momentum leads me to believe that there is another neutral particle taking part in this event. One with enough momentum to fill in the gaps.

One possible hint of which neutral particle this might be is the presence of a $\gamma$ some distance from the strong interaction. There are just a few neutral particles that readily decay into photons. $\pi^0$ and $\eta$.

Suppose that the two negative particles in the bottom prong are $\pi^-$. Using conservation of momentum, the momentum possessed by the neutral particle should be:
$$
	p  =	\begin{bmatrix}
	7772.72 \\
	-30.32
	\end{bmatrix} \implies \lvert p \rvert = 7772.8
$$
The energy in the incoming beam is:
$$
	\approx \sqrt{ 0^{2}+938.3^{2} } + \sqrt{ 9550.38^{2} - 139.6^{2} } \approx 10487.7
$$
The energy contribution from the detectable (charged) particles after the collision is:
$$
	\approx \sqrt{ 117.38^{2} + 139.6^{2} } + \sqrt{ 894.94^{2} + 139.6^{2} } + \sqrt{ 804.70^{2} + 139.6^{2} } + \sqrt{ 67.06^{2} + 139.6^{2} } \approx 2059.7
$$
So, using conservation of energy, the mass of the neutral particle should be:
$$
	\sqrt{ 7772.8^{2}+m^{2} } + 2059.7 = 10487.7 \implies m = \sqrt{ (10487.7-2059.7)^{2} - 7772.8^{2} }
$$
$$
	m = 3258
$$
This predicts that the mass of the neutral particle far exceeds that of either $\pi^0$ or $\eta$. Presumably, however, the uncertainties are quite large. And so I am unsure as to what this particle might be.
